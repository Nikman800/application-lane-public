# Answering common application questions

These notes describe how to answer the questions most applications ask. Every
answer is written in the applicant's own voice, to the employer, and rests on
evidence from the applicant's profile and facts. Keep answers concise: 80–150
words unless the question asks for more or less, or sets its own limit.

## General approach

- Answer the question that was asked, and only that question.
- Lead with the point, then support it with one or two specific examples from
  the profile: a project, a role, a result. Name the real technologies, teams
  and figures the profile states.
- Use the employer's own words where they fit: the product, the mission, the
  skills the posting names. Never copy the posting's claims about the employer
  back as the applicant's own experience.
- Prefer plain, direct sentences over enthusiasm and filler ("I am extremely
  passionate"). One concrete detail is worth more than three adjectives.
- If the profile has nothing that answers a question truthfully, skip it rather
  than writing something vague.

## Why this company / why this role

- Connect something specific the posting says (its product, domain, customers,
  a problem it is solving) to something real in the applicant's background or
  stated interests.
- One clear reason, backed by one piece of evidence, is better than a list.
- If no job text is available, do not guess what the company does: skip.

## Tell us about yourself

- Two or three sentences: who the applicant is professionally now, the most
  relevant experience for this role, and what they are looking for next.
- Choose the material most relevant to this posting; this is not a full
  history.

## A challenge, conflict or failure (behavioral questions)

- Use STAR: the Situation and Task in a sentence, the Actions the applicant
  personally took (the bulk of the answer), and the Result.
- Pick one real story from the profile. Keep its details with that story;
  never blend two experiences into one.
- State results only as the profile states them. If no result is recorded,
  describe what was learned or what changed, without inventing figures.

## Strengths and weaknesses

- Strengths: name one or two that the role needs, each with a short example
  from the profile.
- Weaknesses: choose a real, modest area for growth that is not central to the
  role, and say what the applicant is doing about it. Avoid disguised boasts
  ("I work too hard").

## Career goals

- Describe a direction that fits this role: the skills the applicant wants to
  deepen and the kind of work they want to grow into.
- Stay grounded in the applicant's stated preferences; do not promise a tenure
  or a career path they have not described.

## Salary expectations

- Use only the numbers the applicant has stated in their profile or
  preferences, exactly as given (hourly or yearly, as the question asks).
- If the applicant has stated no number, skip the question; never estimate a
  figure or quote a market range on their behalf.

## Availability, start date, relocation, work authorization

- Answer only from what the applicant has stated, faithfully and briefly.
- If the applicant has not said, skip the question.
- Add no logistics to answers whose questions do not ask for them.

## Experience with a specific tool or skill

- If the profile shows it, describe where it was used and what was done with
  it.
- If the profile shows it only partly (coursework, a side project), say so
  plainly.
- If the profile does not show it, say honestly that it is not part of the
  applicant's background yet and mention the closest real experience. Never
  claim it, and never promise how quickly it could be learned.
